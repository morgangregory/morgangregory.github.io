Fantasy Football Project

Introduction

In this project, I want to look into fantasy football statistics to find better strategies when drafting your team and confirm/deny several “myths” among fantasy football players.

In fantasy football, a group of ~6-14 “owners” take turns drafting teams of real-life NFL players. Each game, individual NFL players are awarded “fantasy points” depending on their performance. Usually, a fantasy team is comprised of 1 Quarterback (QB), 2-3 Running Backs (RB), 2-3 Wide Receivers (WR), 1 Tight End (TE), 1 Defense (D), and 1 Kicker (K). The data I will be using in this analysis uses points per reception (PPR) scoring, which awards an extra point to players every time they catch a pass. For more information on the standard PPR scoring format, look here.

The 5 main questions that I want to answer are:
1: Is the best real-life team also the best fantasy team?
2: Is having several players from the same NFL team on your fantasy team a bad idea?
3: What is the relative value of each position in fantasy football, and how can you use this information to make better drafting decisions?
4: Is there any correlation between a player’s age and their fantasy football performance? Are older players more likely to get injured during the season?
5: How has positional value changed over time?

To do this, I will be using fantasy football scoring data from the 2023 NFL season. In part 5, I will also use data from the past 10 seasons, including the data we have so far for this year.

In fantasy, however, many formats have individual offensive players but defense is represented by one “player” consisting of a whole team’s defensive performance.

Part 3: Positional Value

Next, I would like to explore the value of each (offensive) position in Fantasy Football. First, let’s look at how many points each position scores, on average. Since most fantasy football leagues have 8-12 people, each with ~12-15 players on their team, I will only be looking at the top 180 fantasy scorers. I am excluding Kickers and Defense for this ranking because in general, these positions are drafted much later in fantasy drafts and have much less of an impact on the overall strategy of drafting.

Importing Data:

We can see that WRs and QBs have more players scoring in the 20+ range, and there appears to be two RBs who are the only players at their position scoring 20+ PPG. TEs are the lowest scoring position by a wide margin, with no TEs scoring more than 15 PPG. Here is another look at this data:

QB has the highest Q1, Median, and Q3 PPG out of the 4 positions, but notice that the highest scoring individual player is a RB, followed by a WR. Now, let’s look at the relationship between position and overall fantasy ranking. This fantasy ranking is based on the number of points a player scores and how much better they are than an average player at their position.

ggplot(Position_data, aes(x = Rk, y = PPR, color = FantPos)) + geom_point(size = 2.5) + theme_bw() + theme(legend.title=element_blank()) + xlab("Overall Fantasy Rank") + ylab("Points Per Game (PPG)")

The top ranked players tend to be mostly RB’s and WR’s, with a handful of QB’s and TE’s mixed in. Since there are a larger number of WR’s who average 10+ points than there are RB’s, it may be a good idea to prioritize RB’s in your fantasy football draft. For TE’s, it makes sense to ensure you get a top-8 player, because the rankings and average points scored per game decrease significantly after that range.

When we use the average points scored per game, we see that the value slowly increases before peaking at ~30, and then drops off.

For WRs and RBs, points scored slightly trend upward with age, and for QBs, points scored slightly trends down. TEs stay just about the same. All of these relationships are very weak, however, so age does not have a very strong impact on fantasy points scored.

Next, let’s see how injuries effect players as they age. For this, I will only be looking at the top 120 fantasy players. I am cutting out 1/3 of our sample size because many of the lower-ranked players missed time because they weren’t starting for their team, not because they were injured. A limitation of the data set is that I do not have information on why a player didn’t play a game, only the number of games they played in.

AgeData_Top <- AgeData[1:120, ]
AgeData_Top_Avg <- AgeData_Top |> group_by(Age) |> summarize(MeanG = mean(G))
ggplot(AgeData_Top_Avg, aes(x = Age, y = MeanG)) + geom_point() + theme_bw() + ylab("Mean # of Games Played")

Mean number of games played trends down until age 28, before spiking back up again. To get a better look at injuries specifically, I will filter out players who missed 0 or 1 game. This is more likely to capture injury trends, since a player missing more than 1 game is most commonly due to a prolonged injury.

AgeData_Inj <- AgeData_Top |> group_by(Age) |> filter(G <= 15) |> summarize(MeanG = mean(G)) 
ggplot(AgeData_Inj, aes(x = Age, y = MeanG)) + geom_point() + geom_smooth(method = "loess", se = FALSE) + theme_bw() + ylab("Mean # of Games Played")

Based on this scatterplot, it looks like players who get injured are more likely to miss more time the older they are, up until age ~28. I suspect that the spike in games played among players who get injured after age ~30 is due to the small sample size of players who are age 30+ as we saw in our previous histogram. Also note that this dataset is only consisting of the top 120 overall fantasy players, so players who have a very long / season ending injury would not show up here.

In conclusion, it seems as though age has little to no impact on fantasy points scored, regardless of position. There is some evidence that out of fantasy-relevant players who get injured, they tend to miss more time the older they are. Overall, you shouldn’t be too afraid to draft older players in fantasy football, as they tend to score just about the same number of points than younger players.

The most important aspect of a fantasy football draft is the first round. A good first round pick will set you up well for the season, while a sub-par pick is a detriment to the strength of your team. So, let’s look how the positions of the top 10 fantasy players each year has changed over time to get a better sense of what position should be targeted in the first round.
